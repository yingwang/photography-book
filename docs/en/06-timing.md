# Chapter 6 · Timing: Anticipation and the Decisive Stance

> Great timing in photography is rarely a reflex reaction to something that has already happened; it is the calm anticipation of something about to occur.

---

## 6.1 The Mechanics of Anticipation

Human reaction time—from visual perception through neural processing to finger actuation—averages around 200–250 milliseconds. Add to this the camera's shutter lag (20–50ms).

**If you press the shutter only when you see the decisive moment peak with your eyes, you have already missed it.**

Capturing the apex of action requires **Predictive Framing**:
1. **Identify the Stage**: Find an area of dramatic light, strong architectural geometry, or an intriguing billboard backdrop.
2. **Pre-visualize the Actor**: Anticipate what kind of movement, figure, or gesture would complete the compositional triangle.
3. **Lock Focus & Exposure**: Eliminate all technical latency by setting manual exposure and zone focus in advance.
4. **Release at the Threshold**: Press the shutter a split-second *before* the pedestrian steps into the golden sliver of light.

## 6.2 Micro-Gestures and the Decisive Stance

In candid human photography, the difference between an ordinary snapshot and a masterpiece lives in fractions of an inch:
- The floating extension of a running foot above a rain puddle (Cartier-Bresson's famous *Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare*);
- The momentary turning of a head that aligns a profile with a dark shadow background;
- The subtle, unforced parting of lips in conversation before laughter fades into a flat expression.

Timing is not about frantic hyperactivity; it is about cultivating stillness so that when the micro-gesture appears, your hand executes with relaxed, instant precision.

---

## 6.3 Burst Mode: Tool vs. Psychological Trap

Modern mirrorless cameras can shoot at 20, 30, or even 120 frames per second. While high-speed continuous bursts are indispensable for fast wildlife and sports action, in street and documentary work, relying on indiscriminate burst spraying can cripple your creative growth:
- **Culling Paralysis**: Sifting through 3,000 identical frames at home creates mental exhaustion, dulling your editorial sharpness.
- **Lazy Observation**: When you assume the camera will "catch it somehow," you stop actively reading body language and tension in the field.

**The Golden Rule**: Use short 2–3 frame micro-bursts to capture subtle micro-expressions, but always initiate the burst based on deliberate anticipation, not mindless spray-and-pray.

---

## 6.4 Zone Focusing: Zero Latency on the Street

To eliminate autofocus hunting completely in fast-moving street environments:
- Set your lens to manual focus.
- Stop down to **f/8 or f/11**.
- Set your focus distance ring to approximately **2.5 to 3 meters** (on a 28mm or 35mm lens).
- Everything from roughly **1.5 meters to infinity** will fall within acceptable depth of field.
- When an unexpected moment erupts, you can raise the camera and trip the shutter instantly—zero autofocus delay.

---

## 6.5 Field Practice

1. **The Stage & Wait Drill (Fishing Method)**: Find an exquisite pocket of high-contrast afternoon light on a sidewalk with an interesting background. Set your framing, exposure, and focus. Wait patiently for 30 minutes without moving. Do not shoot random passersby; release the shutter only when a figure whose shape, speed, or clothing completes your composition walks through the light beam.
2. **The Stride Apex Drill**: Practice capturing walking pedestrians so that both feet are suspended mid-stride in an elegant triangle, rather than flat on the pavement. Train your eye to anticipate the heel strike.
